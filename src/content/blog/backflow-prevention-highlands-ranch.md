# The Annual Backflow Certification Highlands Ranch HOAs Forget.

Of every compliance obligation an HOA board carries, backflow certification is the one most likely to be discovered by a letter from the water utility rather than a calendar reminder. It's a small brass assembly, usually in a vault or a mechanical room nobody visits, doing a job nobody thinks about — right up until the annual test lapses and the utility sends a notice threatening to shut off water to the community.

For a Highlands Ranch HOA managing irrigation systems, fire lines, and shared water infrastructure across a large community, backflow is worth understanding before the notice arrives.

## What a backflow preventer actually does

Your community's water system is connected to the municipal drinking-water main. Water is supposed to flow one direction: from the main, into your property, out to the sprinklers, irrigation, and taps. A backflow preventer is a mechanical assembly that makes sure water can *only* go that direction.

The reason matters. Under certain conditions — a water main break, a fire drawing heavy flow from a hydrant, a pressure drop in the municipal system — the normal pressure can reverse. When it does, water that's already gone out into your irrigation lines, your fire sprinkler pipes, or a chemically treated system can get siphoned *backward* into the public drinking water. That's a cross-connection, and it's how fertilizer, stagnant fire-line water, or worse ends up in a neighbor's tap.

The backflow assembly's check valves slam shut when flow reverses. That's the whole job, and it's why the water utility — not just the fire department — cares intensely about whether yours works.

## Why an HOA has more of these than it realizes

A single-family home might have one small backflow device. A Highlands Ranch HOA community typically has several, and boards are often surprised by the count:

- **Irrigation backflow preventers** on every separately metered common-area landscape zone — and a large master-planned community has many
- **Fire-line backflow preventers** protecting the sprinkler systems in clubhouses, amenity buildings, and any common structures
- **Domestic backflow** on shared buildings

Each one is a separate assembly with its own annual test and its own certification filed with the water utility. When a board thinks "we don't have a backflow device," what's usually true is that nobody has ever counted them — and several are quietly overdue.

## The annual test — what it involves and why it needs a certified tester

Backflow assemblies require annual testing by a certified cross-connection control tester. This isn't a visual check. The tester attaches a calibrated gauge to the assembly's test cocks, isolates it with the shutoff valves, and measures whether the check valves and the relief valve hold pressure and open at the right thresholds. The assembly either passes to spec or it doesn't.

Two things make this a job for a certified tester specifically:

- **The certification has to be filed with the water jurisdiction.** The test result isn't just for your records — the utility requires the certification on file, tied to the specific assembly, dated within the year. A test by an uncertified person doesn't satisfy the requirement even if the device is fine.
- **A failed assembly needs repair or replacement, not just a note.** Rubber check-valve seats wear out, relief valves foul, freezing damages assemblies — and Highlands Ranch winters are hard on any assembly in an above-grade or shallow vault. A failed test starts a clock to repair and re-test.

## Where HOAs get caught

The pattern is consistent across communities:

- **Nobody owns the calendar.** The board rotates, the management company changes, and the every-year-in-the-same-month cadence gets dropped. The utility's notice becomes the reminder.
- **The count is wrong.** The community tests the two assemblies everyone knows about and misses the three on irrigation zones added when a new common area was landscaped.
- **The fire-line assembly falls between vendors.** The irrigation backflow gets tested by the landscape contractor; the fire-line backflow — which is part of the fire protection system — gets forgotten because it's not clearly anyone's job. The fix is unglamorous: inventory every backflow assembly on the property, put them all on one annual schedule, and use a certified tester who files the certification with the water utility for you.
