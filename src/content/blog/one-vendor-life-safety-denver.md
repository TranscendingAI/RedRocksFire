# Why Denver Property Teams Stop Juggling Fire Protection Contractors.

Pull the fire protection file on almost any Denver commercial building and you'll find the same thing: a sprinkler contractor, a separate alarm company, an extinguisher vendor who shows up once a year with a tagging gun, a monitoring account nobody remembers setting up, and a backflow tester the water utility told you to hire. Five vendors, five invoices, five sets of paperwork, and five phone numbers — and when the Denver Fire Department flags a deficiency that crosses two of those systems, not one of those companies will own it.

That gap is where compliance problems live. This is a look at what the one-vendor life safety model actually changes for a property team, and why Denver's building stock makes the split-vendor approach especially painful.

## The problem isn't the vendors. It's the seams between them.

Each of those five contractors probably does competent work inside their own lane. The trouble starts at the boundaries.

Your [fire alarm system](#) and your [sprinkler system](/fire-sprinkler-service-and-systems) talk to each other. When a sprinkler flow switch trips, it signals the alarm panel, which signals the [monitoring company](#), which dispatches Denver Fire. That chain runs through equipment owned by three different vendors. NFPA 72 requires you to test the interface between those systems — not just each system on its own. So who tests the handoff?

In a split-vendor setup, the honest answer is usually nobody. The sprinkler company tests to the flow switch and stops. The alarm company tests from the panel and assumes the input is fine. The monitoring company confirms it received *a* signal during a scheduled test window. Each one signs off on their piece. The seam between them — the part that actually matters when there's a fire — goes unverified from one year to the next.

Denver's Fire Prevention Division doesn't inspect the seam either. Their annual occupancy inspection checks that systems exist and appear maintained; it isn't a functional test of every integration point. That responsibility sits with you, the property owner, and it's exactly the responsibility that falls through the cracks when five companies each own one-fifth of the system.

## What the one-vendor model changes

When one company inspects, services, and monitors every life-safety system in the building, the seams stop being someone else's problem. They become the whole point.

- **One team tests the integrations, not just the components.** The flow-switch-to-panel-to-dispatch chain gets verified end to end because the same technician owns both ends.
- **One report, formatted for your AHJ.** Denver Fire has a preferred deficiency-list format, and Denver Fire Code Section 901.6.3 requires inspection and testing records to stay on the premises using NFPA or Denver Fire forms with the service provider's Denver license number legible. One vendor means one record set that already matches what the inspector expects — not five reports in five formats you have to reconcile before an inspection.
- **One phone number when a system goes down.** Denver requires you to report a compromised alarm or sprinkler system, and if the outage runs past 10 hours you need a fire-watch permit. When the clock is running, you don't want to be figuring out which of five vendors handles the impaired system.
- **One accountable party.** When something is wrong, there's no vendor-to-vendor finger-pointing. The answer to "whose problem is this?" is settled before the question comes up.

## Why Denver makes this worse than most cities

Denver's building stock layers a century of development, and that variety multiplies the coordination burden. A converted RiNo warehouse now used as a brewery and event space, a downtown high-rise with a standpipe and fire pump and voice evacuation, a Capitol Hill apartment building with legacy detection, a Central Park medical office with a modern integrated stack — each has a different mix of systems, and each tenant improvement can quietly change the picture.

A new wall in a LoDo restaurant can obstruct a sprinkler head. A change of use in a Five Points storefront can trigger new alarm-notification requirements. A build-out at a Cherry Creek retail space can disable a system that never gets re-commissioned. In a split-vendor world, the sprinkler company doesn't know the alarm company was on site, and neither of them knows the tenant moved a wall. The one-vendor model is the only arrangement where somebody actually holds the whole building in view.

The Denver Fire Department also requires local licenses for the people who inspect and test regulated systems, and construction permits for most system modifications. Coordinating that licensing and permitting across five vendors is its own job. Consolidating it under one is the difference between managing a program and managing a pile of contractors.

## What to ask before you consolidate

If you're evaluating the one-vendor approach for a Denver property, three questions separate a real single-source partner from a company that's just bundling invoices:

1. **Do your own technicians hold current Denver Fire Department licenses for every system you inspect?** Subcontracted work reintroduces the seams you were trying to eliminate.
2. **Do you test the interfaces between systems, and does your report document those tests?** Ask to see a sample report and look for the sprinkler-to-alarm integration test specifically.
3. **Will your report land in Denver Fire's preferred format the first time?** Rework because a report didn't match the AHJ's expectations is the hidden cost of a vendor who doesn't work the jurisdiction regularly.
