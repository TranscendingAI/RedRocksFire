# Wet, Dry, or Antifreeze: Which Sprinkler Belongs in a Fort Collins Building.

In a warm climate, choosing a sprinkler system type is mostly an engineering footnote. In Fort Collins, it's a decision with a failure mode. Water sitting in a pipe that drops below freezing doesn't quietly stop working — it expands, splits the pipe, and floods the building the moment it thaws. Northern Colorado's winters, unheated warehouse space, exterior canopies, and parking structures make sprinkler type a real question with a real wrong answer.

Here's how the three common water-based systems differ, where each one belongs, and what that means for a property under the Poudre Fire Authority.

## Wet-pipe systems: the default, where it stays warm

A wet-pipe system is exactly what it sounds like — the pipes are filled with water, all the time, right up to the sprinkler heads. When a head reaches its activation temperature, the fusible element opens and water flows immediately. No delay, no mechanism between the water and the fire.

Wet systems are the default for a reason: they're the simplest, the cheapest to install and maintain, and the fastest to activate. The catch is the one that matters in Fort Collins — the water in the pipe must never freeze. That means wet systems belong only in reliably heated space: occupied offices, retail interiors, apartment units, conditioned corridors.

The failure case is a wet system whose piping runs through a space that *seems* heated but isn't reliably so — an attic above the insulation line, a stairwell that loses heat, a soffit on an exterior wall. Those are the spots where a burst pipe surprises an owner who assumed the whole building was warm.

## Dry-pipe systems: air in the pipe until it's needed

A dry-pipe system fills the piping with pressurized air instead of water. Water is held back at a dry-pipe valve in a heated space. When a head opens, the air pressure drops, the valve trips, and water flows into the system and out the open head.

That air gap is the whole point: because there's no water sitting in the exposed pipe, there's nothing to freeze. Dry systems are the standard answer for unheated warehouse and industrial space, loading docks, parking garages, and attics — which describes a large share of Fort Collins's industrial and distribution stock.

Dry systems buy freeze protection but cost you two things:

- **A delay.** Air has to bleed out before water arrives, so there's a lag between head activation and water on the fire. System design accounts for it, but it's real.
- **More maintenance.** Dry systems carry additional NFPA 25 testing — trip tests, low-point drain checks to clear condensation that would otherwise freeze and corrode, and air-pressure monitoring — on top of what a [standard annual sprinkler inspection](/blog/annual-fire-sprinkler-inspection-centennial/) already covers. A dry system is not a wet system you can ignore. It's a wet system with more moving parts, all of which the Colorado climate stresses.

## Antifreeze systems: a wet system that resists freezing

An antifreeze system is a wet system charged with an antifreeze solution instead of plain water, so it can occupy exposed space without freezing while still delivering the immediate activation of a wet system.

Antifreeze loops are common for small unheated areas hanging off a larger wet system — an exterior entry canopy, a walk-in cooler vestibule, a short run into an unconditioned space. They avoid the delay of a dry system in that one zone.

The tradeoff is precision. The antifreeze concentration has to sit in a specific window: rich enough to protect against the lowest expected temperature, but not so rich that it won't flow or, in older listed solutions, becomes a combustion concern. That's why NFPA 25 requires a concentration test on antifreeze loops at inspection. A loop that was mixed correctly ten years ago can drift, get diluted by a repair, or fall out of listing as the standards evolve. This is the system type most likely to be quietly out of spec on a property that hasn't had a thorough inspection.

## How to tell which one you should have

Walk the building and ask one question at each run of pipe: *is this space reliably heated in January?*

- **Reliably heated interior** → wet pipe.
- **Unheated warehouse, garage, attic, dock, large exposed area** → dry pipe.
- **Small unheated zone hanging off a warm wet system** → antifreeze loop, or extend dry coverage.

The complications are the in-between spaces and the buildings that changed use. A Fort Collins warehouse converted to climate-controlled offices may have a dry system it no longer needs, carrying maintenance cost for freeze protection that's now redundant. A heated building that added an unconditioned storage annex may have wet pipe running somewhere it will eventually freeze. This is where a walk-through by someone who reads the whole system pays for itself — the same coordinated approach we bring to [warehouse and industrial properties](/industries/warehouse-industrial) across Northern Colorado.

## What Poudre Fire Authority expects

Whatever system type you have, the annual obligation runs through NFPA 25, and Poudre Fire Authority reviews the results. Dry and antifreeze systems carry more test items than wet systems, so the inspection scope should visibly change with the system type. If a report on a dry-pipe warehouse looks identical to a report on a wet-pipe office — same tests, same length — someone skipped the trip test and the low-point drains.
