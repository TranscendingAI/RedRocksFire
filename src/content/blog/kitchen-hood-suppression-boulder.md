# Kitchen Hood Suppression: A Compliance Guide for Boulder Restaurants.

The suppression system tucked into the exhaust hood over your cooking line is, pound for pound, the most heavily regulated equipment in a Boulder restaurant. It's also the system most likely to quietly fall out of compliance — because it sits above the line where nobody looks, it activates maybe once in the life of a restaurant, and the rules governing it have teeth that older systems don't meet.

Here's what compliance actually requires, why the standard matters, and what Boulder Fire-Rescue looks for.

## What the system does, and why grease is the whole problem

A commercial kitchen fire is almost always a grease fire, and a grease fire behaves differently from anything else. It burns hot, it climbs into the exhaust duct where a water line can't reach, and it reignites. A standard sprinkler is the wrong tool. That's why cooking lines get a dedicated wet-chemical suppression system: nozzles aimed at each appliance and into the duct and plenum, charged with a potassium-based agent that both smothers the flame and chemically reacts with hot grease to form a foam barrier that stops reignition.

When the system trips — by fusible link at the hood or by a manual pull — it dumps the agent and, critically, shuts off the gas or electric to the appliances at the same time. Fuel off, agent on. That interlock is part of what gets inspected.

## NFPA 96 and UL 300 — the two standards that govern you

Two references define compliance, and Boulder restaurant owners should know both by name.

- **NFPA 96** — the Standard for Ventilation Control and Fire Protection of Commercial Cooking Operations. This is the umbrella: it requires an automatic fire-extinguishing system for commercial cooking equipment that produces grease-laden vapors, and it governs the hood, the duct, the plenum, and the cleaning cadence.
- **UL 300** — the listing standard the suppression system itself must meet. This is the one that catches older restaurants.

UL 300 came about because commercial kitchens changed. Modern high-efficiency fryers and appliances run hotter and hold more oil than the equipment older dry-chemical systems were designed to protect. A pre-UL-300 dry-chemical system may not put out — or keep out — a fire in a modern deep fryer. So the current requirement is a UL-300-listed wet-chemical system.

If your Boulder restaurant is in an older space with a suppression system that predates UL 300, it may be grandfathered for now — but that grace tends to end at a change of ownership, a kitchen remodel, or a lease renewal, at which point the AHJ will require the upgrade. Finding that out during a planned inspection is far cheaper than finding out during a build-out when the whole opening timeline is on the line.

## The semiannual inspection — this system is on a 6-month clock

Most fire protection systems run on an annual cadence. Kitchen hood suppression does not. NFPA 96 requires the suppression system to be inspected and serviced **every six months** by a qualified contractor. At each service, a technician:

- Verifies the nozzle placement matches the current appliance layout (see below — this is where restaurants get caught)
- Checks and replaces fusible links on schedule
- Confirms the agent cylinder charge and the pressure
- Tests the manual pull station and the automatic actuation
- Verifies the gas/electric shutoff interlock actually trips
- Confirms nozzle caps are intact and blow-off caps are in place
- Tags the system with the service date

The six-month interval is not optional and not something Boulder Fire-Rescue treats as flexible. A system tagged more than six months ago is a finding.

## The deficiency that catches almost everyone: nozzles that no longer match the line

Here is the single most common way a compliant system becomes a non-compliant one without anyone touching the suppression equipment: the kitchen rearranges the cooking line.

Suppression nozzles are aimed at specific appliances in specific positions. Move the fryer, swap a six-burner for a griddle, add a char-broiler, slide the range 18 inches to fit a new prep table — and the nozzles are now aimed at empty air while the new appliance sits unprotected. The suppression system looks fine. It's fully charged, recently tagged, and completely useless for the fire it's most likely to face.

Any time the cooking line changes, the suppression system has to be re-evaluated and often re-nozzled. This is the coordination gap that a restaurant running a general contractor for the remodel and a separate suppression vendor almost always misses.

## What Boulder Fire-Rescue checks

Boulder Fire-Rescue enforces NFPA 96 and the International Fire Code as adopted in Colorado. On a restaurant inspection, expect them to look for a current suppression tag within the six-month window, nozzles aligned to the actual appliances, an intact fusible-link and pull-station setup, a working fuel shutoff, [Class K portable extinguishers](/services/portable-fire-extinguishers) in the kitchen as backup, and evidence the exhaust hood and duct are being cleaned on the NFPA 96 schedule for your cooking volume.
