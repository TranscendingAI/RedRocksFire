# What 24/7 Fire Alarm Monitoring Actually Buys You.

A fire alarm panel that isn't monitored does exactly one thing when it detects a fire: it makes noise. In an occupied building during business hours, that's enough to get people out, which is the point. But most commercial fires that cause catastrophic loss don't start at 2 p.m. in a full building. They start after hours, in an empty one, where a horn on the wall alerts no one and the fire has all night to grow.

That gap between *detection* and *response* is what 24/7 central-station monitoring fills. Here's how it actually works, what NFPA 72 requires, and what it means for an Aurora commercial property.

## The signal chain, step by step

When people say "monitoring," they mean a specific chain of events that happens in seconds:

1. **A device detects.** A smoke detector, heat detector, sprinkler flow switch, or manual pull station activates.
2. **The panel processes.** The fire alarm control panel registers the initiating device, sounds local notification, and — this is the part that matters — sends a signal off-site.
3. **The central station receives.** A UL-listed monitoring center staffed around the clock receives the signal, sees which building and which zone, and verifies per protocol.
4. **Dispatch.** The central station notifies the fire department — Aurora Fire Rescue for a property inside the city — and works down your emergency contact list.

The horn on the wall handles step one. Monitoring handles two through four. Without it, an after-hours fire in a vacant office relies on someone driving past and noticing. With it, the fire department is en route while the fire is still small.

## What NFPA 72 requires — and it's more than "have monitoring"

NFPA 72, the National Fire Alarm and Signaling Code, governs the whole system, and monitoring compliance has parts owners routinely overlook:

- **Supervised communication.** The path from your panel to the central station has to be supervised — meaning if the connection is cut or fails, that failure itself generates a signal. A monitoring link that can go dead silently is not compliant, and it's the classic failure of old copper phone-line setups as carriers retire them. Many Aurora properties still have alarm communicators dialing out over a landline that no longer exists the way it did when the system was installed.
- **Secondary power.** The panel needs backup power — batteries — sized to run the system through a utility outage and still have capacity to sound an alarm. Those batteries have a 4-to-5-year service life, and a failed battery load test is one of the most common alarm deficiencies we find.
- **Annual functional testing.** Every initiating device and notification appliance gets functionally tested on the NFPA 72 cadence, and the monitoring signal itself gets verified end to end — that the central station actually received the right signal from the right zone.

That last point is the one split responsibilities tend to drop. Testing the smoke detector and testing that the central station received the detector's signal are two different tests. Both are required. Only one gets done if the alarm servicer and the monitoring company aren't the same team.

## The signal that never arrives

The failure mode worth designing against is quiet: a system that detects and alarms locally but whose off-site signal never lands. The panel does its job, the horn sounds in an empty building, and nobody is notified because the communicator failed months ago and the failure was never supervised.

This is why the communication path, the secondary power, and the verified end-to-end signal test aren't paperwork items. They're the difference between a monitored building and a building that only appears monitored. ## What monitoring covers beyond fire

A capable central station doesn't only watch the fire alarm. The same monitoring infrastructure typically covers:

- **Sprinkler waterflow and valve tamper** — so a closed valve or a flowing [sprinkler system](/fire-sprinkler-service-and-systems) generates a signal, not just a wet floor discovered Monday morning
- **Security systems** — intrusion and access alarms
- **Elevator recall and emergency phones**
- **Area of refuge two-way communication** — the call stations that give someone who can't use the stairs a lifeline during an evacuation

## What to check on your Aurora property

Three questions tell you whether your monitoring is real or nominal:

1. **How does your panel communicate to the central station — and is that path supervised?** If the answer is an old phone line, verify it still works the way the system assumes.
2. **When was the monitoring signal last tested end to end?** Not the devices — the signal actually arriving at the central station from each zone.
3. **When were the panel's backup batteries last load-tested?** If nobody knows, they're overdue.
