# What Actually Happens During an Annual Sprinkler Inspection.

When a property manager schedules an annual fire sprinkler inspection, the mental image is often a technician walking in, glancing at the riser, and leaving a tag on the way out. That's not the inspection. That's the last thirty seconds of it.

A real annual inspection references NFPA 25 — the Standard for the Inspection, Testing, and Maintenance of Water-Based Fire Protection Systems — and it's a sequence of specific tests with specific pass/fail criteria. Here's what actually happens when we inspect a sprinkler system in Centennial, and what South Metro Fire Rescue Authority expects to see documented when it's done.

## Before anyone touches the system

The inspection starts with the paperwork, not the pipe. We pull the previous inspection report and the system's original design records. Two things drive the scope of everything that follows:

- **Which installation standard the system was built under** — NFPA 13 for full commercial, 13R for low-rise residential. That determines what "correct" looks like for this specific building. (If you manage apartments or condos, our [guide to sprinkler standards in multi-family properties](/blog/fire-sprinkler-systems-in-multi-family-properties/) breaks down 13 vs. 13R vs. 13D in detail.)
- **What the last inspection flagged.** A deficiency noted last year that's still open is a different conversation than a clean prior report.

South Metro adopts NFPA 25 along with the International Fire Code, and their Community Risk Reduction division reviews the reports. Knowing their preferred deficiency-list format before we start means the finished report lands right the first time.

## The main drain test

This is the single most informative test in the inspection, and it takes about ten minutes.

We open the main drain valve, let the system flow, and read two pressure numbers: static (before flow) and residual (during flow). Then we compare those readings against the last inspection.

A drop in static pressure between years is the tell. It points to a supply-side restriction — a control valve that's been partially closed somewhere down the line, internal pipe scaling, or in the worst cases a curb-side valve the water utility left partly shut after work in the street. The main drain test is how a system that *looks* fine reveals that it can't actually deliver water where it counts. A sprinkler system that can't move water is a decoration.

## Valve supervision — every valve, physically

Every control valve on the system has to be in the open position and either locked open or electronically supervised. This sounds trivial. It is the source of some of the most dangerous deficiencies we find.

A single closed valve can isolate an entire wing of a building from its water supply, and there's no visible sign inside the protected space — the heads are still on the ceiling, the pipe is still there, and none of it will do anything in a fire. So we physically visit every valve, confirm its position, check that it's locked or supervised, and tag it. We don't take the panel's word for it.

## Gauges, heads, and the visual inspection

- **Gauges.** Every pressure gauge has a calibration window. Expired gauges get replaced and the readings logged, because a gauge you can't trust is worse than no gauge.
- **Sprinkler heads.** We look for corrosion, paint, dust loading, and physical damage. In Centennial's older Arapahoe Road office parks and in unconditioned attic spaces, painted and corroded heads are the most common finding. A painted head may not activate at its rated temperature — the paint insulates the fusible element.
- **Obstructions.** Storage racks, signage, tenant build-outs, and — every December — holiday decorations installed too close to a deflector. South Metro cites obstructed heads more than almost anything else. We measure clearance to the inch.
- **Hazard changes.** If a tenant remodeled a unit and changed what the space is used for — added a [commercial kitchen](/blog/kitchen-hood-suppression-boulder/), converted to storage, blocked a sprinkler path — the original system may no longer match the hazard. That gets flagged.

## Dry, preaction, and antifreeze systems add steps

Plenty of Centennial properties have coverage in unheated attics, parking structures, or exterior canopies, which means dry-pipe, preaction, or antifreeze systems. Those carry extra testing: trip tests, low-point drain checks, and — for antifreeze loops — a concentration test to confirm the mixture still protects against freezing without being so rich it won't flow. Skipping those on a dry system is skipping the part most likely to fail in a Colorado winter.

If you want the deeper breakdown of how these system types differ, we cover it in our [wet vs. dry vs. antifreeze guide](/blog/wet-dry-antifreeze-sprinkler-fort-collins/).

## The deliverable

The inspection ends with a written report, and the report is the product. A passing report includes:

- Each test result in plain English, next to the actual value we read
- A photograph of every deficiency — a deficiency described without a photo is what you'd get from someone who didn't actually go look
- Findings ranked by urgency: critical items to correct within days, non-critical within about 90 days, and record-keeping notes
- A correction summary formatted to South Metro's expectations, so it goes straight to the AHJ without reformatting
- The inspector's signature and certification number

That report is what your AHJ, your insurer, and a future property manager all rely on. It's also what stands between you and a rework cycle when the fire marshal reviews the file. (More on [what a report your AHJ will actually accept](/blog/inspection-report-arvada-ahj/) looks like.)

## When to schedule it

Two rules of thumb for Centennial properties:

1. **At least 90 days before your AHJ renewal.** That leaves room to correct anything the inspection turns up before South Metro reviews the file.
2. **Coordinate it with your [alarm](/services/fire-alarm-service-and-systems), [extinguisher](/services/portable-fire-extinguishers), and [backflow](/services/backflow-prevention-assemblies) inspections.** One coordinated visit means one round of tenant coordination and one reconciled record set instead of four.
