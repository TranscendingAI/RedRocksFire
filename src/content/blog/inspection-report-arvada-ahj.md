# The Inspection Report Your Arvada AHJ Will Actually Accept.

Two inspection companies can test the exact same building, find the exact same problems, and hand you two completely different reports. One goes straight to the Arvada Fire Protection District, gets accepted, and closes out your compliance for the year. The other bounces — the AHJ wants clarification, wants findings organized differently, wants the photos it doesn't have — and you're now managing a rework cycle for an inspection you already paid for.

The walkthrough is the commodity. The report is the product. Here's what separates a report your AHJ accepts on the first pass from one that generates a second round of work.

## The report is the only part the AHJ sees

This is the fact that reframes everything: the fire marshal almost never watches the inspection. They read the report. Every judgment they make about whether your building is compliant, whether a deficiency is serious, and whether you've corrected it — all of it comes from a document, not from having been there.

Which means a report can be technically accurate and still fail, if it's organized in a way that makes the AHJ's job harder. The Arvada Fire Protection District, like every AHJ, has a way it wants to receive information. A report that respects that gets processed. A report that ignores it gets questions.

## What a first-pass report contains

A report the AHJ accepts without follow-up has a consistent anatomy:

- **Every test result in plain language, next to the value.** Not "system tested — satisfactory," but the actual reading: the static and residual pressure from the [main drain test](/blog/annual-fire-sprinkler-inspection-centennial/), the battery load-test result, the specific gauge readings. A reviewer can trust a number they can see; they have to question a verdict they can't verify.
- **A deficiency list ranked by severity.** Findings sorted into what's critical (correct now), what's non-critical (correct within a defined window), and what's record-keeping only. An AHJ reading a flat, unranked list has to triage it themselves, and reviewers don't love doing the inspector's prioritization for them.
- **A photograph of every deficiency.** This is the single biggest differentiator. A deficiency described in a sentence with no photo asks the AHJ to take your word for it. A deficiency with a clear photo — the obstructed head, the corroded valve, the failed FDC cap — is self-evident. It also protects you: it's dated proof of the as-found condition.
- **A correction summary in the AHJ's expected format.** The list of what needs fixing, structured the way the Arvada Fire Protection District structures it, so it maps directly onto how they track corrections. This is the piece that most often causes a bounce when it's absent or formatted for a different jurisdiction.
- **Inspector credentials and signature.** NICET or CSA certification number, signature, and contact for follow-up. A reviewer with a question needs a name and a number, not a company general line.

## The tell of a report that will bounce

You can spot a weak report before the AHJ does. The warning signs:

- **Verdicts without values.** Page after page of "satisfactory" with no readings behind it.
- **No photos, or photos of the building instead of the deficiencies.** A hero shot of the riser proves nothing; a photo of the specific corroded head proves the finding.
- **A flat deficiency list with no severity ranking.** Everything presented as equally urgent, which means nothing is.
- **Generic formatting.** A report clearly built for a different jurisdiction, or a national template that ignores how Arvada wants its corrections tracked.

A report like this can describe a perfectly good inspection. It'll still cost you a rework cycle, because the AHJ can't act on it cleanly.

## Why local matters here

The reason a locally engaged inspection company hands you a first-pass report is simple: they work the jurisdiction every week. They know the Arvada Fire Protection District's preferred deficiency-list layout because they've submitted to it repeatedly. They know which findings Arvada treats as immediate and which get a correction window. A company parachuting in from outside the area can run a flawless test and still produce a report that reads wrong to the AHJ, because they've never had to satisfy this reviewer before.
