# Getting Ahead of Colorado Fire Code Changes.

Fire codes are not written once and left alone. The model codes — the International Fire Code and the NFPA standards it references — are revised on multi-year cycles, and every Colorado jurisdiction decides on its own when to adopt each new edition. Which means the rules that govern your Westminster property this year may not be the rules that govern it in two years, and the change won't announce itself. Most property managers find out a code changed the way they find out most things: at an inspection, from a finding.

There's a better way to run it. Here's the playbook for staying ahead of code changes rather than getting caught by them.

## Why "the fire code" is a moving target

Two things make code compliance a moving target instead of a fixed checklist.

**The model codes get revised.** The International Fire Code publishes on a three-year cycle. NFPA standards — 13 for sprinklers, 72 for alarms, 25 for inspection and testing, 10 for extinguishers, 96 for kitchen suppression — each revise on their own cycles. Each revision can change requirements: new editions have tightened sprinkler requirements in certain occupancies, changed inspection cadences, added requirements for things like in-building radio coverage, and revised what counts as an acceptable system.

**Jurisdictions adopt on their own timelines.** Here's the part that surprises people: a new code edition isn't in force in Westminster the day it's published. It's in force when the jurisdiction *adopts* it, often with local amendments. So Westminster, Denver, and Boulder can be enforcing different editions of the same standard at the same time. A requirement that's current in one Front Range city may be a year or two away in the next. Denver, for instance, publishes its own building and fire code based on a specific IFC edition with local amendments — a reminder that "the Colorado fire code" is really a patchwork of jurisdictional adoptions.

For a property manager with buildings in more than one jurisdiction, this is the trap: assuming a compliance posture that's fine in one city carries over to a property in another. It often doesn't.

## The two ways code changes reach your building

When a jurisdiction adopts a new edition, it hits your property through two doors:

1. **New construction and modifications** get held to the current adopted code immediately. Any build-out, system modification, or change of use is reviewed against whatever edition is in force now — not the edition your building was built under. This is where most managers first encounter a new requirement: a tenant improvement that used to sail through review now triggers something new.
2. **Existing buildings** are usually not forced to retroactively meet every new requirement — existing systems are typically grandfathered — but not always. Some code changes are made retroactive for life-safety reasons, and the grandfathering ends the moment you do a significant renovation or change the occupancy. A building that's been fine for a decade can trigger current-code compliance the day you start a major remodel.

The practical upshot: you don't get to ignore code changes just because your building already exists. You get to defer some of them until the next time you touch the building — and then they all come due at once.

## The playbook

Staying ahead of this is a matter of a few deliberate habits.

- **Know which editions your jurisdiction currently enforces.** Not the model code — the specific editions the Westminster Fire Department has adopted, with local amendments. This is knowable, and it's the baseline for everything else.
- **Get a heads-up before you renovate.** The most expensive code surprises happen mid-project, when a build-out review turns up a current-code requirement nobody budgeted for. A pre-project review against the *current* adopted code — before design is locked — turns a mid-construction crisis into a line item you planned for.
- **Treat adoption cycles as scheduled events, not surprises.** Jurisdictions telegraph adoptions in advance. A partner who tracks the Westminster adoption calendar can tell you what's coming and what it means for your portfolio before it's in force.
- **Coordinate across your portfolio's jurisdictions.** If you manage properties in Westminster, [Denver](/blog/one-vendor-life-safety-denver/), and beyond, you're managing multiple code postures at once. One vendor tracking all of them beats each site figuring it out alone.
- **Lean on your inspection reports as an early-warning system.** A [good inspector](/blog/inspection-report-arvada-ahj/) flags not just what's non-compliant today, but what's *going to be* a problem under the edition that's coming. That forward look is worth as much as the current-year findings.
